Context

Conservation Background

Cambodia has expanded protected areas significantly since the 1990s as part of broader biodiversity conservation efforts. Despite legal protection, many conservation areas continue to face pressure from logging, land concessions, infrastructure projects, and fires — a tension between formal protection and ongoing development that frames any analysis of Tatai Wildlife Sanctuary.

The sanctuary lies in the Cardamom Rainforest, a biodiverse wilderness and an area of interest for Cambodia's goal to reintroduce tigers. It was designated a protected area in May 2016 (144,247 ha) and amended to 144,539 ha in July 2023. The site has faced poaching, logging, fires, and land grabbing; satellite records also document deforestation across Cambodia's protected areas as recently as 2022.

Management initiatives include ecotourism planning with a sustainability focus. Portions of the landscape participate in the Southern Cardamom REDD+ Project, aimed at reducing emissions, halting deforestation, and combating forest degradation.

Protection zones

Cambodia applies four zone types within Tatai:

  • Core Zone — 42,872 ha (~30%) · access heavily restricted
  • Conservation Zone — 54,045 ha (~37%) · no exploitation; limited public access
  • Sustainable Use Zone — 18,558 ha (~13%) · regulated development and resource use
  • Community Zone — 29,064 ha (~20%) · local resource use under a management plan

Methods

Data & Processing

  • Study area: Tatai Wildlife Sanctuary, Koh Kong Province, southwestern Cambodia (Cardamom Mountains).
  • Satellite data: Landsat 8/9 Collection 2 Level-1 imagery — December 2013, 2020, and 2025.
  • Seasonal control: Dry-season scenes selected to minimize cloud cover and seasonal vegetation variation.
  • NDVI: Calculated from Landsat multispectral bands to measure vegetation greenness.
  • Change detection: NDVI difference analysis (2013–2025) via ArcGIS Pro Raster Calculator.
  • Fire data: NASA FIRMS alerts (Aqua/Terra MODIS, 2015–2025) with Global Forest Watch forest-loss context.
  • Roads: OpenStreetMap shapefiles via Geofabrik — 2020 vs 2025 network comparison.

Mean NDVI decreased slightly from 0.403 (2013) to 0.397 (2025). The small magnitude suggests overall vegetation greenness remained stable after the 2016 conservation designation. Spatial difference analysis shows most areas experienced minimal change or slight increases; the largest negative changes occur in water bodies, which naturally register low NDVI. Reports of illegal logging and wildlife activity may explain concentrated negative changes away from water.

Overall vegetation conditions appear largely stable across the study area.

NASA FIRMS data (2015–2025) yielded 285 fire alerts from Aqua and Terra MODIS — not all confirmed as active fires. Global Forest Watch analysis shows 150 ha of forest loss from fire between 2016 and 2024, compared with 41 ha between 2005 and 2015. Total tree cover loss across both periods was 3.2 kha; fires accounted for a fraction of total loss but appear to have had the strongest effect on vegetation health.

Although not every alert represents confirmed fire damage, several alert clusters align with areas of lower vegetation vigor on the NDVI map. After 2015, alert counts dropped — potentially reflecting protected-area status — yet recent fires may have had greater per-event impact. One hypothesis: increasingly extreme wet/dry seasons under climate change may make forests more fire-prone even as alert frequency declines.

Stressors

Hydropower, Roads & Human Activity

Stung Tatai Hydropower Project

  • Small NDVI decline (2013–2025) likely linked to dam construction in the northern conservation area (completed 2014, funded by China National Heavy Machinery Corp).
  • High-NDVI forest converted to open water (low NDVI), pulling down sanctuary-wide averages.
  • NDVI increases downstream may reflect reduced water flow exposing previously submerged land.
  • Outside the dam footprint, NDVI remained stable or increased — decline reflects reduced vegetation extent, not widespread health loss.

Road & access pressure

  • No reliable human-activity baseline exists for 2013, limiting attribution of early road effects.
  • OSM road layers (Geofabrik, 2020 vs 2025) show network expansion across and around the sanctuary.
  • Areas with new roads overlap zones of negative NDVI change on the difference map.
  • Ecotourism suitability mapping identifies hotspots in the south, central, and northwest — as development expands, human traffic will likely increase.
Findings

Conclusion

  • NDVI decreased slightly from 2013 to 2025, but the overall change was very small — vegetation health remained largely stable across most of the sanctuary.
  • Most of the area showed stable conditions or slight vegetation growth, indicating resilient large-scale ecological conditions.
  • The strongest loss occurred near the Stung Tatai hydropower reservoir and in localized fire-affected areas, matching the 2013–2025 change map.
  • Observed changes were localized rather than widespread — major impacts tied to specific disturbances, not broad ecosystem decline.

What worked

  • NDVI and change detection clearly identified where landscape change concentrated over time.
  • Cross-referencing fire alerts, road expansion, and hydropower helped explain localized declines.
  • Multiple ancillary datasets placed NDVI patterns in broader environmental context.

Limitations

  • Small NDVI differences and limited human-activity data made separating causes difficult.
  • Water bodies strongly lower NDVI, amplifying negative values near reservoir expansion.

Future work

  • Deeper analysis of hydropower expansion, fire history, and land-use activity to isolate drivers of localized change.
